2024 – now · DeezerCase study

AI music detection

Spotting songs made by AI among the ~200,000 tracks uploaded to Deezer every day.

AudioProductionResearch
My roleSenior ML Engineer, leading the production initiatives
In collaboration withResearchers, ML engineers and software engineers
StackPyTorch, GCP, BigQuery, Spark, Airflow, Kafka, Kubernetes, Grafana

The problem

AI generators like Suno and Udio made it possible to generate a full song in seconds. Uploads of fully AI-generated tracks to Deezer grew from about 10% of daily deliveries in January 2025 to more than half at the June 2026 peak. Left unchecked, they dilute recommendations and the royalty pool, and listeners can't tell the difference.

How detection works

Upsampling layers in AI music generators leave a trace in the audio they generate: small peaks at perfectly regular intervals in the frequency spectrum, which we call a fakeprint. Our ISMIR 2025 Best Paper explains where they come from, and the core detection model builds on this idea.

What I built in production

I lead the production initiatives: taking the system from research prototype to a service that scans every new release, and keeping it accurate as new generators appear.

My contributions

  • Packaged and deployed the core fingerprint model, processing ~200K tracks a day.
  • Trained and deployed multi-class source classifiers covering 7+ generators.
  • Reached >99.5% recall at <0.01% false positive rate by scaling the training database 10× and adopting modern deep learning techniques.
  • Defined the rules engine behind the "AI-generated" album tag, now on 14M+ albums.
  • Built drift monitoring and retraining pipelines, and streamlined research-to-production so the full 200M-track catalog can be retrained on and processed in 30 minutes.

Research I brought to internal tools

Detection has to keep up with people trying to evade it and with new generators. I deployed internal tools based on three recent Deezer Research methods, which our team uses for real-time manual investigation. Pick one to see how it works.

A simple trick fools most AI-music detectors: speed the track up or slow it down. Every frequency is stretched by the same factor, so the telltale peaks move away from where the detector expects them. I deployed internal prototypes of a Deezer Research method that solves this by design.

Not every AI track is fully generated. Producers increasingly mix an AI-generated vocal over a human-made beat, or the other way around. Separating the stems first doesn't help, because separation wipes out the artifacts. A Deezer Research method, which I deployed internally, keeps the detector on the full mix and uses separation only to measure how loud each stem is, chunk by chunk.

A detector only knows the AI generators it was trained on, and new ones come out every few months. To find them, we cluster new uploads by their fakeprint, with no labels. Tracks from the same generator end up together, so a new one shows up as its own cluster. I deployed the tool the team uses to review these clusters every week.

2024 – now · Deezer

Stable music communities for recommendation

Can we identify communities of tracks that stay together from one week to the next in the collaborative embedding space, and what do they tell us about the structure of our embedding space?

RecommendationContent understanding
MethodsSVD embeddings, orthogonal Procrustes, k-means, graph community detection (Louvain, Leiden, InfoMap)

The problem

Every week, Deezer computes SVD embeddings from a matrix of track co-occurrences in playlists. The embedding space is then cut into 5,000 clusters with k-means. Those clusters power Flow, and Flow Tuner, where listeners pick the genres they want.

This setup raises three issues.

  • The embeddings move every week, and the clusters move with them. Search indexes have to be updated with each new set of embeddings. What happens if embeddings and indexes fall out of sync across servers?
  • Many clusters have no genre label, which keeps them out of Flow Tuner. Can we find regions of the space stable enough to label first?
  • Clusters ignore the structure of the space and over-partition it. The silhouette score points to around 600 clusters rather than 5,000, so dense regions get split arbitrarily.

Step 1 · Aligning embeddings to find stable tracks

SVD runs from scratch every week, so embeddings from two successive weeks can't be compared directly. The same track can get different coordinates even if its neighbors barely changed. To compare the two spaces, both weeks' embeddings have to be aligned.

We do this with Orthogonal Procrustes, which already runs in production. It finds the rotation R that best maps next week's embeddings onto this week's:

R* = argminR ‖ Xt+1 R − Xt ‖F   with   RᵀR = I

Because R is orthogonal, it can rotate or flip the space but not distort it, so angles and norms are preserved. After alignment, we can compare each track with its position the week before and see which tracks moved.

  • The first dimensions of the SVD are stable up to a sign flip. Deeper ones are noisier.
  • Popular tracks align best: the most streamed tracks keep the most neighbors.
  • Recent tracks are less stable on average.
  • Tuning the alignment doesn't help much.
588K+tracks are extremely stable
0.03%of streams at risk during a server desynchronization

So the embeddings hold up well, and a short desynchronization would affect very few streams. That raises the next question: are the clusters built on top of them just as stable?

Step 2 · Group the clusters into communities

The clusters are less stable than the embeddings they're built on. k-means draws borders through dense regions, so tracks near a border often switch clusters from one week to the next.

We group neighboring clusters into larger communities: a track that changes cluster usually stays in the same community. We link clusters whose centroids are close, then let a community detection algorithm find groups of clusters that belong together.

We compared Louvain, Leiden and InfoMap, and kept InfoMap, which gave the most stable communities: ~1,200 communities from the 5,000 clusters. Flow keeps using the same clusters: communities sit on top as a second level.

Result

A track is "stable" if it stays grouped with most of the same tracks from one week to the next. Measured that way (stability threshold 0.85):

1.2Kcommunities grouping the 5K clusters
0.62recall of stable tracks with communities, vs 0.38 with clusters
~2×more stable tracks: ~1.25M vs ~600K
  • Stable over time. The gain holds over several consecutive weeks, not just two.
  • Flow is untouched. Communities sit on top of the existing clusters as a second level.

Communities that mean something

Communities often share a genre, a language or a country, even though they were built only from listening patterns. Some revealed regional genres missing from Flow Tuner; others showed existing genres whose coverage could grow. Editors can now label them.

Ask for music in your own words

A prototype that turns requests like "upbeat songs for a rainy Sunday" into a playlist.

ResearchPrototyping

The problem

People want to ask for music the way they'd ask a friend: "upbeat songs for a rainy Sunday" or "something like my Friday playlist, but calmer". Large language models can understand such requests, but they're too slow and costly to run for every listener.

What I did

  • Prototyped Just Ask for Music (JAM), a lightweight Deezer Research approach to natural-language, personalized music recommendation.
  • Rebuilt the datasets and pipelines for production, optimized the models, and tested what breaks on real-world requests.
  • Deployed a working query-to-playlist prototype.

2024 · Hugging FaceCase study

Diarizers: who spoke when?

An open-source library for building speaker diarization models.

Speaker diarizationEngineering

The problem

Speaker diarization answers "who spoke when?" in a recording: essential for meeting notes, podcasts and call analysis. Open models exist (pyannote), but adapting them to a new language or domain is hard, and conversations labeled with every speaker's timestamps are rare and expensive.

What I built

  • Lead developer of Diarizers, a library to fine-tune pyannote diarization models with the Hugging Face ecosystem. On Japanese phone calls, about 5 minutes of fine-tuning on a single GPU cut the error rate from 25% to 18%.
  • Published fine-tuned models in 5 languages and diarization datasets on the Hub (diarizers-community), with tutorial notebooks.

Making synthetic training data

Since labeled conversations are scarce, I built a pipeline that generates synthetic conversations from single-speaker recordings, which are available in large quantities in almost every language.
